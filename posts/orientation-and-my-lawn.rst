Some of my best mathematical thinking occurs when I am doing mundane things.
Last night I was mowing my lawn.  One of the things I like about living in the
suburbs is that I have a lawn.  Another thing I like about living in the suburbs
is that my lawn only takes 30 minutes to mow.

After mowing, the next task is trimming the edges that the mower can't reach. So
I have to get out the string trimmmer and walk around the edge of the lawn.  My
back yard is surrounded by a fence, a deck, and a retaining wall, so I have to
stand on the grass to trim the edge.  There's also a tree in the middle of the
lawn, surrounded by a mulch ring, and I have to trim around that as well.

I trimmed the edge of the lawn, and then I trimmed around the tree.  Afterwards,
I noticed that I had gone clockwise around the edge, but counterclockwise around
the tree.  Why was that?

Quickly, I figured it out.  The boundary of a region in the plane is a set of
curves.  When orientation is taken into account, boundary curves on the interior
inherit the opposite orientation than those on the outside.  If I wanted to trim
around the tree clockwise, I would have had to have held the trimmer on the opposite
side of my body than the side that feels natural to me.

Astute Calculus III students will notice that the usual orientation on the
boundary of a region is the one that goes counterclockwise from above.  So I was
following the opposite convention by going clockwise around the edge of the
lawn.  The explanation for that is simple, too:  I am left-handed.
