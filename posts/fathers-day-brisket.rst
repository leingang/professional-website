    Last March Seabra's had whole brisket on sale, so I bought one.  The first thing
    I learned was that "whole" in the circular means "WHOLE", as in, the whole cut
    and not sliced into family-sized portions.  But they were nice enough to cut
    it into pieces, which went straight into my freezer.

    I decided to smoke one up for Father's day, with delicious consquences.

.. TEASER_END

Brisket is the cut of beef from which corned beef is made.  This is what I kept
telling my kids, because they love corned beef but they wrinkled their noses
at the sound of the word “brisket.”  Smoked brisket as barbecue is popular in Texas,
where they have always had plenty of excess beef.

After defrosting I rubbed the piece down with the Southern Dry Rub for Barbecue
from the Joy of Cooking.  Active ingredients: paprika, cumin, salt, pepper, brown sugar.
It happened to be in the refrigerator.

I put in on the grill about 6:00am.  On one side, the heat.  I used an iron
smoker box full of hickory  wood chips that had soaked overnight.  On the other
side, the meat.  I put an aluminum pan under the brisket, and poured in the
water than the  chips had soaked in.

I kept it on there for just over 10 hours at about 250 degrees.  I didn't get a
whole lot of smoke from the chips.  I've had better results with a foil packet.

When I took it out it was nice and barky.  The thermometer read 165 on the
bottom and 175-180 near the top. I know that 190 is the magic number to break
down meat, but I wanted to give it time to rest, and I was really afraid of  it
being dry.  Plus I've never owned a thermometer that I had complete faith in.

After this I tried the “FTC” method they rave about on the BBQ forums.  FTC
stands  for *foil*, *towel*, *cooler.*  It is a method for resting meat that
keeps it from cooling down too much.  Resting meat is important to let the juices
distribute evenly, but let it rest on the counter for too long and it will get
too cool to enjoy.

You usually think of a cooler as something to keep things cold.  But it's just
an insulator, and not smart enough to know the difference between keeping heat
from coming in versus keeping heat from getting out.  So something warm in the
cooler stays warm (not forever—the contents will tend towards room temperature,
just like they would if they were cold.)  The foil keeps the juices from
escaping, and the towel provides further insulation.

I wrapped the meat up tightly and bundled it in two old dishtowels (you don't want
to risk leaking greasy beef juice on good towels).  I managed to get it in one of
Igloo Playmate coolers that holds about a sixpack.  I figured the snug fit would
be better for heat retention.

For sides, I made some Texas-style pinto beans and cornbread.  Neither were sweetened,
as you might find with southern barbecue.  Also a cucumber salad with dill and a
red wine vinaigrette.

When I took the brisket out of the cooler and sliced it, I know it was going to
be heavenly.  The knife went right through the top half (the “point”) with very
little resistance.  The bottom (“flat”) was a little less tender, but I kept
the slices think and across the grain.  They glistened with juice and fat rendered
from the center ribbon.

Served!
